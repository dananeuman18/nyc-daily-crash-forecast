Introduction This project examines daily motor vehicle collisions in New York City using time-series analysis and forecasting techniques. The objective is to identify long-term trends, weekly and annual seasonal patterns, and changes in collision frequency. The analysis evaluates Seasonal Naïve, Exponential Smoothing (ETS), Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA), ARIMA with explanatory variables (ARIMAX), and Time Series Linear Models (TSLM). Forecast performance is evaluated using a chronological training and testing design. Observations from 2012 through 2024 are used for model estimation, while 2025 is reserved for out-of-sample evaluation. #2. Conclusion This project examined daily motor vehicle collisions in New York City using historical data from July 2012 through December 2025. The primary objective was to identify long-term trends, weekly and annual seasonal patterns, and changes in collision frequency while evaluating the forecasting performance of several statistical models. The exploratory analysis identified meaningful differences in collision frequency across the days of the week. Fridays recorded the highest average daily collision count at 524.2, while Sundays recorded the lowest at 393.4. The weekday-versus-weekend comparison also showed higher average daily collisions on weekdays (490.09) than on weekends (420.34). These findings suggest that weekly travel patterns are associated with differences in daily collision frequency. Stationarity testing using the KPSS test produced a test statistic of 28.927 and a p-value of 0.01, providing evidence against the null hypothesis of stationarity. This finding supports the use of appropriate time-series modeling techniques to account for temporal patterns and changes in the series. Six forecasting models were evaluated using a chronological training and testing design. Historical observations through December 2024 were used for model estimation, while the 2025 observations were reserved for out-of-sample evaluation. Among the six forecasting specifications, ETS Automatic achieved the best overall forecasting performance, recording an RMSE of 28.77, an MAE of 23.33, a MAPE of 10.05%, and a MASE of 0.4101. ARIMA Automatic ranked second, with an RMSE of 29.59 and an MAE of 23.48. The results indicate that ETS Automatic provided the most accurate forecasts during the 2025 testing period. The TSLM analysis also provided insight into the explanatory contribution of trend and seasonal variables. The Trend-Only model achieved an R-squared of 0.5074, the Trend-Weekly model achieved 0.5523, and the Full TSLM model achieved 0.5681. These results indicate that incorporating weekday indicators and annual Fourier terms increased the proportion of historical variation explained by the models. However, the Full TSLM model did not produce the best out-of-sample forecasting accuracy. Motor vehicle occupants accounted for 553,385 reported injuries, followed by pedestrians with 141,606 and cyclists with 68,863. These findings demonstrate differences in the distribution of reported injuries across road-user categories and emphasize the importance of considering multiple groups when evaluating urban traffic safety. Overall, the project demonstrates the value of time-series analysis in understanding historical collision patterns and evaluating alternative forecasting approaches. ETS Automatic emerged as the strongest forecasting model based on the 2025 testing results, while the TSLM analysis provided useful insight into the contribution of long-term trends and seasonal patterns. The findings provide a statistical foundation for further research into urban traffic patterns, collision forecasting, and road-user safety in New York City.
